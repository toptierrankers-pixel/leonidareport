GTA History

GTA III: The Game That Changed Everything

Leonida Report Staff

Grand Theft Auto III's 2001 release is one of the most important moments in video game history. It took the top-down chaos of the first two games and rebuilt it in full 3D, and in doing so, effectively invented the modern open-world genre.

A genuine technical leap

Liberty City was, for its time, an astonishingly dense and alive-feeling world. Pedestrians reacted to the player, traffic followed rules that could be broken in satisfying ways, and the city felt genuinely explorable rather than a collection of level-select missions strung together, which was the norm for most games at the time.

A silent protagonist and a dark tone

Claude, GTA III's protagonist, never spoke a single line, letting players project themselves fully into the role. Combined with a genuinely dark, betrayal-driven story and a satirical radio talk show underpinning the whole experience, GTA III set the tone that every subsequent GTA game has built on.

The controversy that followed

GTA III's violence and moral ambiguity drew significant political and media backlash, becoming a frequent target in debates about video game violence during the early 2000s. That controversy, uncomfortable as it was for Rockstar at the time, only added to the game's cultural footprint.

The legacy

Every open-world game made since, inside or outside the GTA series, owes something to what GTA III proved was possible: that players wanted worlds to explore on their own terms, not just missions to complete in sequence. It remains one of the most influential single releases in the medium's history.
